Baseball hitting academy

Role-play drill on inbound Facebook lead calls
Baseball hitting academy — company name rendered in the transcript as "gradam g swing"
Rob LimeUnclearProspect

Despite the FTC label this is a standing coaching session with an existing client: Tyler brings two instructors, Wilson and Justin, who call Facebook and ticket leads to book free swing evaluations. Rob reframes how much pain, impact and commitment a lead call actually needs — only enough to get a parent in the door, not enough to close a $1,900 program — then runs both reps through live role plays with Tyler playing the dad. He hands them the opening question, the emotional question for dad, third-party close language, and a script for deflecting the early price question, and closes by telling Justin his conviction, not his technique, is the gap.

Not a sales call — a training session that ended with Rob noting both reps improved and the group agreeing to pick it up next time.

Key moments 12❯
4:29
Right-sizing the close
“You still want pain and you want impact and you want commitment, but you only need enough pain, impact and commitment to get them to drag their asses in for the eval.”
The cleanest statement of a principle reps constantly get wrong — the discovery has to be proportional to what you are actually asking for.
5:49
The straightest path to pain
“I would go with, hey, Tyler, what made you put the lead into Facebook in the first place? What made you even want to opt into this conversation you're having with me?”
Shows how to replace a friendly warm-up question with the one that gets the compelling reason in the first ten seconds.
10:12
Stops the role play right before the pitch
“Let me stop you right there, Wilson. You're doing so good. And then let me stop right before we — let's get you guys in.”
Model of when to interrupt a role play: at the exact moment the rep stops asking and starts talking about themselves.
10:58
Remember who writes the check
“Dad's the one that's writing the check, so how dad feels about it is important too. Go ahead and ask him an emotional question too.”
The rep had taken the kid's pain but not the buyer's — a transferable miss in any call with a sponsor and an end user.
11:25
Third-party close instead of an ask
“Typically that same guy would say, you know what, Wilson, I'll take you up on your offer to come in and do a free eval. Is that what you're saying?”
The exact language for letting the prospect opt in rather than asking them for the appointment.
12:08
Tie the appointment back to the pain
“You're not coming in to check out the technology. You're coming in because your kid is hitting the ball on the ground, he's getting out, and he's losing his confidence. That's why you're coming in.”
A one-line fix for reps who close on their own capability instead of the prospect's reason.
15:16
An upgrade from 'would it be worth it to you'
“What is it worth it to you is better than here's what I want to do. But even one up from that might be, hey Tyler, people who tell me this and this and this tell me it would be worth it to come in and do the eval.”
Ranks three versions of the same close from weakest to strongest — easy to steal and teach.
18:05
The gap question
“What do you see in those guys you don't see in junior? It's a real simple way to get him talking directly. That's a nice layup that they give you.”
Turns an evasive 'just wanted to see what you have' into specific pain by using a comparison the prospect raised himself.
21:09
Deflecting price without dodging it
“Hey Tyler, great question. Price is important. We've got a couple of different ways that we do this, but would you mind if we get through the assessment to make sure there's even an opportunity to enroll you guys before we talked about the program?”
Permission-based sequencing language that works for any early price question.
25:36
Conviction, not technique
“You have to be more comfortable and speak with more conviction. They will make comparisons about your ability as an instructor based off of your conviction in this conversation.”
The real diagnosis of the second role play — the words were right and the delivery still cost the call.
27:14
Con is short for confidence
“I hate to say this because I know you guys aren't conning, but the word con is actually short for confidence, because people that are selling bullshit, as long as they're confident, people will buy it.”
A blunt, memorable way to make a rep take delivery seriously without making it about ego.
30:07
If we get impact, they're coming in
“People who haven't used a hitting coach yet, it's just about impact and commitment. If he says junior is struggling with confidence and it sucks to watch him do it, they're coming in.”
Closes the session by collapsing everything taught back to one predictive variable.
