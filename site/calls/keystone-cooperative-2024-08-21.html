Keystone Cooperative

Marketing head wants cold-call muscle for merged energy division
Shad TidlerAdvancedProspect

Amy runs marketing and sales across Keystone Cooperative's four divisions and came in via a referral from Rodney. Her agronomy sellers have plenty of development resources; her energy division — liquid fuels, propane, lubricants sold into construction firms, fleets, municipalities and schools — is six months into a merger and has become order-takers who do not knock on doors. Shad asked for permission to ask questions, traced the order-taker behavior back to two legacy cultures and an incentive plan being rebuilt, then pushed on whether the sellers are even motivated to hunt and whether the energy sales manager would be open to help. He proposed a three-way working session with a pre-work survey and an explicit right to say no.

Amy agreed the deeper three-way meeting was appropriate, said she would raise it with energy sales manager Mike Bradley on their Thursday call, and gave Shad permission to chase her the following Tuesday if he had not heard back.

Coaching notes❯

Shad never asked for a number. Growth is the stated driver, the energy division is the profitable one, and no revenue target, gap, or cost of inaction ever enters the call — so there is no pain-impact to point back to in the next meeting.

The best sequence is 9:51 to 11:22: he repeats her own phrase, then goes straight at motivation rather than skill. That ordering is what sets up the diagnosis-first pitch later.

At 18:37 Amy deflects his close with 'what are your thoughts, how would you approach an organization with this?' and he gives a four-minute overview. It works, but a coach could watch this as a decision point — he could have answered in thirty seconds and gone back to closing.

He got a decision-maker (Mike Bradley) named, confirmed as open, and a dated callback with permission to chase. That is a strong finish for a call with no scheduled meeting on the books.
