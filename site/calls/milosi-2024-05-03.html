Milosi

Kickoff expectations and a sales-process teardown
Rob LimeAdvancedProspectTenure at meeting: Pre

Taylor runs Milosi, a Nashville-area landscape design-build and maintenance company restructuring into separate commercial and residential lines under John and Austin, with roughly six or seven salespeople on each side. Rob spent the first half setting expectations for the engagement: the four-S model, why structure carries the leverage, the learning sequence that precedes behavior change, learn-know-live-own, the change curve, the new toy phase, thirty-day truth-telling check-ins, and a pre-planted referral ask. He then had Taylor screen-share the Milosi process and interrogated it, pressing for milestones, prospect decisions and expectations, and noting that a process this good should not produce their sales-cycle problem. They closed on the May 14 in-person leadership kickoff with the scope set as shortening the sales cycle.

May 14 in-person kickoff confirmed for 7 to 11 a.m. at the Hendersonville office with Austin and John added, scope agreed as shortening the sales cycle, and Taylor to bring his process documents.

Key moments 12❯
8:15
What success actually looks like
“If you can look at your team and go, everybody on the team, or most people on the team, are incrementally turning into better versions of themselves, then the rest of everything else kind of will take care of itself. Revenue is kind of a lagging indicator for that piece of work.”
Sets the measure of the engagement before any numbers are discussed, which is what protects month three.
10:08
Why structure carries the leverage
“The leverage that we have, and how well we take advantage of that leverage, is going to be based off mostly structure, because all the reinforcement mechanisms... I get in front of the room for four hours, and then it's up to the leaders to reinforce that for the next three months.”
The clearest one-sentence case for doing infrastructure before training.
12:03
Why leaders go first, even when the client wants speed
“People go, well, can we get the team started right away? And it's like, God, believe me, I would love to do that. But the team at some point is going to be looking at the leaders, asking questions, looking at the leaders if they're skeptical, looking at the leaders for reinforcement. We just need them five or ten steps ahead of their team.”
Acknowledges the client's impatience as reasonable, then gives the mechanical reason it doesn't work.
14:05
The four stages of evidence before behavior changes
“We'll change the way that they see the world from a sales standpoint. We'll get them increased awareness. Then we'll get them talking a little differently with a common language, whether it's pain or compelling reason or up-front contracts. And then finally we'll get some behavioral change. Leading up to that, it's not that nothing's happening.”
Pre-empts the week-six 'is this working?' conversation by naming what progress looks like before results appear.
17:28
Learn, know, live, own
“You learn an idea and you could start to apply it, but it still requires time, energy, effort, conscious thought. Then you know it, where it becomes habitual. Then you live it, when you've changed your behavior patterns. And then owning is when everything that we teach is innate to you, you use it in your personal life, you use it to get a table at a restaurant.”
Explains a four-year average client relationship as a function of depth rather than dependency.
21:36
Structure the hell out of three months
“We tend to structure the hell out of like three months at a time. But the problem, especially with a new client, is that you might not be able to see ninety-one days into the future. So you don't want to be so structured that you have no fluidity for when you're thirty-one days in and it's like, we need to do more of this and less of this.”
The direct answer to Taylor's structured-versus-fluid question, and it concedes the real limit instead of promising a system.
27:12
The change curve, and the dip people misread
“If we're sixteen days in and someone blows something up that used to be pretty easy, their instinct is going to be, I shouldn't have done that, this doesn't work. When the reality is, you didn't do the thing that we asked you to do very well, therefore we got a negative outcome.”
Inoculates the owner against the first bad result, which is usually when training programs get quietly abandoned.
28:30
New toy phase and telling each other the truth
“There's this moment where it's new stuff, fun, new toy, and that wears off, and it'll get difficult, and we'll get into the dog days of application of technique. Sometimes that's day thirty-one, sometimes it's day sixty-one. You and I will get together and tell each other the truth every thirty days.”
Names the predictable slump and attaches a standing meeting to it in the same breath.
29:25
Plants the referral ask before any work is done
“People that are happy with the work that we're doing together, that they're seeing the results they expect to see from us, make introductions and referrals for me. So I just, if you're cool with that, I'm going to ask you for that in the future.”
Asks for permission to ask, at the one moment when there is nothing to be awkward about.
33:58
Three questions that turn a flowchart into a sales process
“So what are the milestones associated with each? To advance an opportunity. And then also, what decision does the prospect need to make to advance? And then, what expectations are we setting with them? Do we have any of that?”
The most portable moment on the recording. Works on any client's process document.
42:26
Interrogating the process out loud
“I'm interrogating the process here. A lot of what you just told me is actually pretty good, but they can't be doing all that super well, or else you wouldn't have the sales cycle issue, right?”
Cuts into a guided tour with a contradiction, and it gets Taylor to locate the real bottleneck in design and estimating rather than in selling.
52:57
The $50,000 end table
“People ask about price early for two reasons: because they want to disqualify you, or because they don't want to be embarrassed when you give them the number. I went into a furniture store, and there was an end table, and the guy told me fifty. And I was like, there's no way it's fifty dollars. And he was like, sir, it's fifty thousand. And I felt like a stupid hick. You have to protect people's okayness.”
A story Taylor can hand straight to his designers, aimed at exactly the moment he had just described as their weakness.
