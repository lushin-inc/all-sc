Precimac

Negotiating-gambit training for an aerospace machining team
Brian KavickyUnclearSalesTeam

Precimac sells precision machined parts to aerospace and defense OEMs — Triumph, SpaceX, Intermountain Turbine, Ozark Air Works, Vericore Power Systems — and its reps keep getting worked over by trained purchasing agents on government and OEM programs. Brian Kavicky spends the hour naming eleven gambits (flinch, competition-is-cheaper, lower authority, good cop/bad cop, hot potato, fait accompli, best and final, stonewalling, nibbling, changing the rules, splitting the difference, "you have to do better") and giving a preventive and an in-the-moment counter for each, all built on one rule: pre-decide your walk-away point and be willing to use it. The last eight minutes turn into a working session where the team names what they actually hit, and David lands on using deal-killing language to purge stalled quotes out of the pipeline.

Not a sales call — a recurring training session with an existing client that ends on "all right, let's have a great week" with no next step and no commitment asked for.

4:43
It is a game, and the game means they need you
“A lot of this today, the emphasis is going to be recognizing that a game is being played with you, and respecting that that game is being played for a reason, which is leverage.”
Reframes manipulation from personal insult into information — the whole hour depends on reps hearing a gambit and feeling curious rather than scolded.
5:39
Selling versus negotiating, defined
“Selling is I get what I want on my terms. Negotiation is both parties entering with a perceived posture of win-win, but both parties are trying to actually create a win-lose.”
The cleanest two-sentence distinction in the file, and the premise for everything that follows.
6:30
No pain, no negotiation
“The rule we teach is no pain, no sale. You guys have been getting sales all the time with no pain. It's more: no pain, no negotiation — or less negotiation, or more leverage in the conversation if you have pain.”
He adapts the Sandler maxim on the fly to a quoting business that genuinely does close without pain, instead of insisting the rule applies as written.
8:40
Decide the walk-away before you need it
“First rule is, when negotiating, know your walk away point and be okay with going there if needed. Pre-decide what you're going to walk away from and when, so that when that point comes it's easy for you. If you don't decide, or it's wishy washy, or it's a moving target, that will cause you to be emotional.”
Every counter he teaches later is unusable without this; worth hearing why the decision has to be made in advance.
11:49
Why a flinch actually hurts
“The whole point of that reaction is it unlocks what's called the parent child response.”
He explains the mechanism — disproportionate reaction after a long cooperative stretch — which is what makes reps stop taking price objections personally.
13:53
Princess and the pea: build up to the number
“You ever ride a roller coaster? You get on the roller coaster, you put your seatbelt on, then they pull the lap bar down. I want you to pull your lap bar down. And this roller coaster also goes upside down, so you know the big shoulder harness thing — I want you to put that on, because I'm about to tell you it's expensive.”
A reusable script for taking the air out of a price flinch before the price is ever said.
17:42
Answer "the competition is cheaper" by killing the deal
“The biggest fix in the moment to this is to react by saying: so does that mean you're going to buy it from them?”
The single most repeated move in the hour — go straight to the deal-kill to find out whether the threat is real.
20:51
Never negotiate with someone who cannot concede
“The fix to this is: don't negotiate at all with anybody who can't offer a concession. You'd say, so if we lower our price to this level, does that mean you're committing to buy? And they'll say, well, I can't do that. You say, that's fine — I would like to present that price to the person who can actually commit, so we'll reserve it for that meeting.”
Converts a lower-authority gambit into a path to the decision maker, which is the outcome the rep wanted anyway.
42:28
Turn a nibbler into a non-nibbler
“You turn a nibbler into a non-nibbler by saying: ooh, that's an important thing. By the way, I'm sure there's other stuff. What's all the other stuff we should also be talking about right now?”
Short, polite, and immediately usable; he then adds that naming the tactic out loud is always fair game.
49:31
Whoever names a number first loses
“The rule of negotiation is the person who goes first is always the loser. So get them to go first and say, you know, I'd love to come down on it, I'd love to work with you, but I don't even know what's going to get it done. Can you tell me what gets it done?”
Comes with the reason it matters — salespeople discount themselves further than the buyer would have asked for.
58:59
The team converts it into a decision
“An easier way to get feedback and really just kill the deal and move on, rather than tie up our pipeline with stuff that we're not going to win anyway.”
The one place in the hour where training becomes an action — David applies deal-killing language to his own stalled quote log. Prospect-side payoff.

Coaching notes❯

Filed under FirstTimeConversations but it is not one — no prospecting, no qualifying, no next step. This is a recurring training session with an existing client. Useful for teaching negotiation, not for studying a first conversation.

Fireflies collapsed five named client voices (Jason, Nick, George, David, Hans) into a single "Speaker 1" and misattributed several of their lines to Speaker 2. Read speaker labels loosely; attribute by content.

The structure is the steal: name the tactic, explain the psychology that makes it work, then give both a preventive and an in-the-moment counter. Every counter is the same underlying move — mirror it and go straight to the deal-kill to test whether the threat is real.

The hour only becomes a working session in the last eight minutes, once he stops presenting and asks which gambits they actually see. The live exchange from 51:12 on is worth more than the first forty minutes.

He teaches at 52:30 that "the fix to a stonewall is always to get a next step" and then closes his own session without one. Worth flagging when this clip gets used in training.
